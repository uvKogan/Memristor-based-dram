# Item 2: Is there an independent, primary-source ReRAM interface-speed citation for the book's 800 MHz DIMM interface clock?

**Question this note answers:** The MBMM book runs all ReRAM configurations at an 800 MHz DIMM interface clock, derived only by doubling the 400 MHz basis of the PCM baseline (B. C. Lee et al., "Architecting Phase Change Memory as a Scalable DRAM Alternative," ISCA 2009 - a PCM paper, not a ReRAM paper). Does any primary-source ReRAM paper, datasheet, or vendor disclosure independently state an achievable ReRAM DIMM-level or chip I/O interface clock/data rate that could justify (or contradict) 800 MHz?

**Bottom line up front: No.** No primary source was found - among the book's own already-cited ReRAM references or via independent search - that states a ReRAM-specific DIMM-level or chip I/O interface clock anywhere near 800 MHz. The one candidate that looked promising on its face (EMBER's "100 MHz" macro spec) was checked against the actual paper full text (both publications) and is unambiguously the macro's **internal** array/read-write-circuit operating clock, explicitly distinct in the paper's own text from the macro's **external** command interface (SPI), for which neither EMBER paper states any clock or data-rate number at all. Every genuine, datasheet-stated ReRAM **chip I/O interface clock** found in this search (Fujitsu MB85AS4MT, MB85AS8MT; Adesto/Renesas RM25C family) is a serial (SPI) interface running in the **single-digit-to-tens-of-MHz** range - two to three orders of magnitude below 800 MHz, and if anything a contrary data point, not a supporting one. **The book should disclose 800 MHz as an uncited modeling assumption** (derived by doubling the unrelated PCM 400 MHz figure), not as a literature-backed ReRAM-specific number.

Method note: Both EMBER papers (ESSCIRC 2023 conference version and JSSC 2024 journal version) were already present as full-text PDFs in this repository (`documents/reference_validation_papers/EMBER_A_100_MHz_...pdf` and `documents/reference_validation_papers/EMBER_Efficient_Multiple-Bits-Per-Cell_...pdf`, also duplicated under `resources/papers/`). These were read in full via local text extraction (not abstract-only) for this note - see exact quotes and locations below. The Xue et al. ISSCC 2021 paper and the Jo et al. IEDM 2014 (Crossbar) paper are **not** present locally; for those, only what is stated in abstracts, publisher metadata, or accessible secondary reporting could be checked, and this is flagged explicitly wherever it applies.

## 1. Candidate: L. R. Upton et al., "EMBER..." ESSCIRC 2023 (book ref [6]) - is "100 MHz" an internal clock or an interface speed?

**Verified by full-text read of the actual paper** (`EMBER_A_100_MHz_0.86_mm2_Multiple-Bits-per-Cell_RRAM_Macro_in_40_nm_CMOS_with_Compact_Peripherals_and_1.0_pJ_bit_Read_Circuitry.pdf`, IEEE 49th ESSCIRC, Sep. 2023, pp. 469-472):

- Fig. 2(b), "overview of the macro specifications," lists **"Operating Frequency: 100 MHz"** as one line item alongside Process Node (40 nm), Read Time (12 ns, 2 cycles), Cell Count, Read Voltage, and Supply voltages. This is unambiguously the macro's internal read/write circuit clock: the paper directly ties it to internal timing budgets - "Gbleed forces 99% read current settling to occur by T_max ≈ 4τ_settling, and the sense amplifier clock frequency becomes 1/(2·T_max)... Minimum G_bleed that meets T_max = 5 ns **(100 MHz)** is used" (body text, Section on sense-amplifier design) - and to the write pulse width - "corresponds to between ~0.4 pJ/cell and ~10 pJ/cell per SET/RESET pulse for a 10 ns pulse width (the minimum at **100 MHz**)" (Section III, results).
- The paper separately and explicitly identifies the macro's **external** command interface: "The read/write analog interfaces in EMBER are operated by a digital controller, which receives commands over a **serial peripheral interface (SPI)**." (Section II, architecture description, immediately following the DAC/sense-amplifier description). This SPI interface is mentioned exactly twice in the paper (once in the block diagram label "SPI Interface," once in this sentence) and **no clock frequency, baud rate, or data-rate number is given anywhere in the paper for the SPI interface** - it is described only qualitatively as the command channel.

**Conclusion:** the "100 MHz" in EMBER's title and specs is the array/macro's internal operating clock (governing read latency, sense-amp timing, and write pulse width), not a stated I/O/interface data rate to a host or DIMM. The paper does have an actual external interface (SPI) and gives it no numeric speed at all. There is no basis in this paper for treating "100 MHz" as evidence for, or against, an 800 MHz DIMM-level interface clock - the two are not the same kind of quantity, and the paper does not supply the kind of quantity (an external interface speed) the book's 800 MHz figure would need.

## 2. Candidate: A. Levy et al., "EMBER: Efficient Multiple-Bits-Per-Cell Embedded RRAM Macro for High-Density Digital Storage," IEEE JSSC, vol. 59, no. 7, pp. 2081-2092, July 2024 (book ref [31]) - same question, journal follow-up

**Verified by full-text read of the actual paper** (`EMBER_Efficient_Multiple-Bits-Per-Cell_Embedded_RRAM_Macro_for_High-Density_Digital_Storage.pdf`, DOI 10.1109/JSSC.2024.3387566).

Abstract (p. 2081), quoted exactly: *"The macro operates at 100 MHz with 64k × 48 = 3M cells in TSMC 40-nm CMOS, achieving 1 b/cell read operation with 1.0 pJ/bit energy at 2.4 Gbps, and 2 b/cell read with 1.1 pJ/bit at 1.6 Gbps. 1 b/cell write-verify operates with 0.40 nJ/bit energy at 12.4 Mbps (BER < 6e-4), and 2 b/cell write-verify operates with 1.2 nJ/bit at 3.8 Mbps (BER < 3e-3)."*

The 2.4/1.6 Gbps and 12.4/3.8 Mbps figures might look, out of context, like plausible "interface data rate" candidates. **Checked directly against Section V.A of the paper** (p. 2088, "Read Energy and Bandwidth"), quoted exactly:

> *"EMBER's read bandwidth is 2.4 Gbps for 1 b/cell (48 bits per word, 2 cycles per word, 10 ns per cycle) and 1.6 Gbps for 2 b/cell (96 bits per word, 6 cycles per word, 10 ns per cycle)."*

This is an explicit, checkable arithmetic derivation: 48 bits / (2 cycles × 10 ns/cycle = 20 ns) = 2.4 Gbit/s. **This "bandwidth" is the macro's internal parallel-word readout throughput** - i.e., how fast the array's internal 48-bit-wide (or 96-bit-wide) output word is produced by the internal read circuit running at the 100 MHz internal clock - not a serial per-pin data rate presented at an external chip I/O pad or DIMM data bus. It is mechanically derived from the same 100 MHz internal operating clock already addressed in Section 1 above, multiplied by the macro's internal word width. The same is true of the 12.4/3.8 Mbps write-verify figures (Section V.B, p. 2089: "For 1 b/cell, we achieve 12.4 Mbps write speed at 0.40 nJ/bit... For 2 b/cell, we achieve 3.8 Mbps write speed at 1.2 nJ/bit"), which are write-verify throughput of the internal write/verify loop, not an external interface spec.

Neither "SPI," "interface," "I/O," "host," "DIMM," "DDR," nor "bus" (as an external bus) appears anywhere else in the 12-page journal paper in a context that attaches a clock or data-rate number to the chip's external command/data interface. (A full-text keyword search of the paper for these terms, beyond the SPI mention already covered in Section 1, turned up nothing.)

**Conclusion:** Same as the ESSCIRC paper - the JSSC follow-up's "100 MHz" and its derived Gbps/Mbps bandwidth figures are internal-macro throughput numbers, not external/DIMM-level I/O interface specifications. This paper does not supply an independent ReRAM interface-speed citation either.

*(Note, for provenance: these exact 2.4/1.6 Gbps and 12.4/3.8 Mbps figures were already correctly identified and used elsewhere in this book - see `Project_Book.typ` item (14) in the review-fix log, which uses their ratio (2.4/1.6 = 1.5x) as an MLC read-latency multiplier. That prior use is legitimate for its purpose (an internal relative-latency multiplier between 1-bit and 2-bit/cell modes) and is unrelated to, and does not establish, an external DIMM interface clock number - the two uses must not be conflated.)*

## 3. Candidate: Crossbar Inc.'s 4Mb cross-point array (book ref [9]: S. H. Jo et al., "3D-stackable crossbar resistive memory based on Field Assisted Superlinear Threshold (FAST) selector," IEDM 2014, pp. 6.7.1-6.7.4)

- Crossbar's press release ("CrossBar Unveils Another Breakthrough Innovation Behind its Ultra-High Density 3D RRAM Solutions at IEDM 2014") and the Semiconductor Digest preview describe the FAST selector's **device-level** switching metrics: turn-on/recovery time "< 50 ns," selectivity "> 10^7-10^10," sneak-current suppression "< 0.1 nA" on a "4 Mbit integrated 3D stackable passive crossbar array." **No I/O interface clock, data rate, MB/s, Mbps, or MHz figure for the chip or array's external interface is stated anywhere in the accessible press material.** The "<50 ns" figure is a per-device switching/settling characteristic of the selector element, not an interface or bus clock.
- A separate, unrelated 2013 Crossbar "stealth mode" press release (PRNewswire, Aug. 2013, for Crossbar's RRAM technology generally, not tied to the specific 4Mb FAST-selector array or to book ref [9]) claims marketing figures of "140 megabytes per second" write and "17 megabytes per second" read, compared against flash. This is a company marketing claim, not a peer-reviewed measurement, is not attached to the cited paper, and does not specify whether it is a DIMM-style interface rate or an internal array figure. **It is explicitly excluded from consideration here** - both because it is not from the cited primary source and because, as a bandwidth figure of undisclosed provenance, it cannot be responsibly converted into a "clock" figure or credited as a DIMM interface spec.

**Conclusion:** No usable interface-clock citation found for book ref [9], within what could be accessed. The underlying IEDM 2014 paper itself remains unverified beyond title/authors/venue; this is a genuine gap (paywalled/not locally available), not a "confirmed absence" of an interface-speed claim inside the full paper.

## 4. Candidate: C. Xue et al., "16.1 A 22nm 4Mb 8b-Precision ReRAM Computing-in-Memory Macro with 11.91 to 195.7TOPS/W for Tiny AI Edge Devices," ISSCC 2021 (book ref [8])

This paper is also **not** available locally, and IEEE Xplore itself could not be fetched (returned blocked/empty content on every attempt from this environment). Only abstract-level secondary summaries (Semantic Scholar, ResearchGate listing, ACM DL citing context) were accessible.

- Accessible summaries describe the macro's **compute-in-memory** performance: a stated "14.8 ns computing latency" for an 8-bit MAC operation, and the headline 11.91-195.7 TOPS/W efficiency figures. This is a per-MAC-operation compute latency for the CIM array's analog accumulation, not a clock cycle time and not an external memory-interface data rate; treating "1/14.8 ns" as an implied "~67.6 MHz" interface clock (as one search-engine synthesis suggested) would be a fabricated derivation not stated in the paper and is explicitly **not** used or endorsed here.
- The paper's own subject matter (a low-power ADC-based readout interface for a compute-in-memory macro, "sign-weighted 2T2R array," per its cited abstract) is oriented around MAC throughput and power, not DIMM- or chip-I/O-level data movement, and no interface clock/data-rate figure surfaced in any accessible summary.

**Conclusion:** No usable interface-clock citation found among what could be accessed for this paper; full text (page/section-level detail) could not be verified - flagged as **abstract/secondary-summary only, full text not accessed** (IEEE Xplore paywalled/blocked from this environment).

## 5. Independent search: any other primary source stating a ReRAM chip/array/module I/O interface clock or data rate

Independent search for ReRAM-specific interface-speed citations (vendor datasheets, other ISSCC/ESSCIRC/JSSC/VLSI papers, ReRAM DIMM/module prototypes, JEDEC-adjacent proposals) turned up the following, **all far below 800 MHz** and none reaching DIMM/DDR-class speeds:

- **Fujitsu MB85AS8MT** (8 Mbit SPI ReRAM, commercially released Sept. 2019) - datasheet DS501-00060-2v0-E (2021.01), fetched and read directly in full. "AC Characteristics" table (datasheet page 18): parameter **"SCK clock frequency f_CK"**, Min 0, **Max 10 MHz**. "Features" summary (datasheet page 1): "Operating frequency: 10 MHz (Max)."
- **Fujitsu MB85AS4MT** (4 Mbit SPI ReRAM, earlier generation) - confirmed via a NASA NEPP (NASA Electronic Parts and Packaging Program) Total Ionizing Dose test report (D. Chen, E. Wilcox, K. LaBel, NASA GSFC, Feb. 2017), which states, quoting the manufacturer datasheet directly: *"The MB85AS4MT is a 4 Mbit ReRAM with Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI). The maximum operating frequency is 5 MHz."* (Section II, "Device Under Test," p. 1 of the NASA report). This was fetched and read directly in full; the underlying Fujitsu datasheet itself was not separately located, so this is a datasheet figure as quoted by a secondary (but authoritative, government test-report) source, not the datasheet PDF itself.
- **Adesto/Renesas RM25C family** (RM25C256, RM25C32, serial SPI ReRAM) - found via search-engine summary of the Mouser-hosted datasheet PDF (direct WebFetch of the PDF timed out twice and could not be independently re-verified in this session): maximum SCK clock **1.6 MHz in normal read mode**, **20 MHz in fast-read mode**. This is reported with lower confidence than the two Fujitsu figures above, since it was not independently confirmed by direct document text extraction in this session - flagged as **search-engine-summarized, not independently re-verified from the primary PDF**.
- No ReRAM DIMM, ReRAM DDR-style memory module, or JEDEC-adjacent ReRAM interface proposal was found in any primary source. Searches for "ReRAM DIMM," "ReRAM DDR interface prototype," and "ReRAM 800 MHz" returned only conventional DRAM/RDRAM/DDR results (e.g., unrelated Rambus PC-800 RDRAM and DDR2-800 listings) with no ReRAM connection.
- Weebit Nano's public technology pages describe ReRAM as a licensable IP macro/"memory module" (in the SoC-embedded-IP sense, not a DIMM) with qualitative claims ("100x faster write access than flash") but **no quantitative interface clock or data-rate figure** was found in the accessible pages.

## 6. Overall answer to the task's three questions

**(a) Does a genuine, independently-sourced ReRAM interface-speed citation exist among these candidates or others found via search, that could justify (or contradict) 800 MHz as a ReRAM DIMM interface clock?**

No citation was found that could *justify* 800 MHz. Several genuine, verifiable primary/near-primary sources were found that *contradict* it by showing real, fabricated, datasheet-specified ReRAM chip interface clocks running at 5-20 MHz (Fujitsu, and by secondary report Adesto/Renesas) - roughly 40x to 160x slower than 800 MHz. These are small serial (SPI) discrete ReRAM chips, not DIMM-class parallel memory, so they are not a direct substitute reference for a DDR5-style DIMM interface either; they are cited here only as the best available real-world ReRAM I/O-interface-speed data points, and they point in the opposite direction from 800 MHz, not toward it.

**(b) If yes: exact number and precise citation.**

Not applicable - no supporting citation exists. (The contradicting data points are cited precisely above in Section 5: Fujitsu MB85AS8MT datasheet DS501-00060-2v0-E, "AC Characteristics" table, "SCK clock frequency f_CK... Max 10 MHz," directly verified; Fujitsu MB85AS4MT "maximum operating frequency is 5 MHz," per NASA NEPP TID test report, Section II, p. 1, directly verified.)

**(c) If no such citation exists: state so explicitly.**

**No ReRAM-specific, primary-source citation exists that independently justifies an 800 MHz DIMM-level or chip I/O interface clock for ReRAM.** EMBER's "100 MHz" (both the ESSCIRC 2023 and JSSC 2024 publications, both fully read for this note) is the RRAM macro's internal array/read-write-circuit operating clock, not an interface/PHY speed - the macro's actual external interface (SPI) is given no numeric speed at all in either paper, and it would in any case be architecturally incomparable to a DDR5-style DIMM data bus. The Crossbar/Jo IEDM 2014 paper (ref [9]) and the Xue ISSCC 2021 paper (ref [8]) could not be fully verified (paywalled/blocked; only abstracts and secondary summaries accessible) but nothing in what was accessible states a chip- or DIMM-level I/O interface clock either. The book should disclose the 800 MHz ReRAM interface clock as an **uncited modeling assumption** - derived by doubling the PCM-specific 400 MHz basis from Lee et al., ISCA 2009, a paper about a different memory technology - rather than presenting it as a literature-backed, ReRAM-specific figure.
